Patient · Step 01

One login, the whole family

Signup is a phone number and an SMS code — nothing else. No email, no password, no forms. In a market where one literate family member usually manages everyone's healthcare, the account has to hold more than one person from the very first screen.

What this step establishes

  • Each family member gets a separate, permanent medical file
  • Ayesha books for her mother without pretending to be her
  • The phone number becomes the identity across SMS, WhatsApp and app
Why it matters: if signup demanded an email address and a password, a large share of the people who most need this product would stop here.

Patient · Step 02

Search the way people actually ask

Nobody searches for "cardiology, Lahore." They ask for a good heart doctor in Gulberg, female, under three thousand rupees, who can see Ammi this week. The filters mirror that sentence.

Filters that carry real weight here

  • Area, not just city — crossing Lahore for a follow-up is a half-day
  • Fee ceiling — most consultations are paid out of pocket
  • Doctor's gender — decisive for many patients, not a nice-to-have
  • Language — a Punjabi-speaking doctor changes what an elderly patient can explain

Patient · Step 03

The verified seal is the whole product

An unverified directory is worse than no directory, because it lends credibility to people who haven't earned it. So verification isn't a badge bolted on later — it's the thing patients are actually buying, and unverified listings must look visibly, uncomfortably different.

What the seal claims

  • The registration number was checked against the national register
  • The qualification shown matches that registration
  • It is re-checked on a schedule, not once at signup
Tap "Check her registration" to see the stamp land. Note the third listing: cheapest fee, vague specialty, no verification. That contrast is the directory's moral argument.

Patient · Step 05

Reach the patient where they already are

Push notifications assume the app is installed and notifications are allowed. For a large share of patients here — particularly older ones — neither is true. WhatsApp and SMS aren't fallbacks in this product; they're the primary channel, and push is the bonus.

What each channel is for

  • WhatsApp — the 24-hour reminder, with reply-to-confirm
  • SMS — guaranteed delivery for the day-of details
  • Push — live changes: doctor running late, slot moved
The commercial argument: reminders are the cheapest no-show reduction available, and no-shows are the number every clinic owner feels. This screen is how you sell the platform to doctors.

Patient · Step 06

The visit, and the slip that survives it

Two things happen on the day. Before the consultation, the patient can see their queue position instead of guessing — no more three hours in a crowded waiting room. After it, the prescription goes straight into their file.

Where the paper used to go

  • Into a plastic bag, then lost before the follow-up
  • Handwritten, then misread at the pharmacy
  • Never seen again by any other doctor

A digital prescription fixes all three at once — and quietly builds the record that makes the next section possible.

Tap the button to watch the prescription get stamped into the vault. That single action is the platform's real flywheel: every consultation makes the patient's file more valuable, which makes leaving harder.

Patient · Step 07

The record is hers, and she decides who reads it

This is the feature that makes CareConnect more than a booking app. A patient walking into a second opinion with a complete, structured history is a fundamentally different patient — she isn't repeating tests, guessing at drug names, or being told to start again.

Three controls, all in her hands

  • Scope — one condition, or everything
  • Duration — a fixed window, or until she revokes it
  • Audit — who opened the file, and when

Note the mix of sources: doctor-written records sit alongside a photographed paper parchi from March. Patient upload is what makes the vault useful before a single clinic has signed up.

Try it: toggle the scope, pick an expiry, create the link. Nothing here is shared by default, and nothing is permanent. No clinic can lock a patient's history inside its own system.

Doctor · Step 01

Nobody lists until they're checked

The doctor's first experience of the platform is a gate, deliberately. A profile that isn't verified isn't visible — which means the directory's quality doesn't depend on anyone's good behaviour after the fact.

Decisions to settle before build

  • Who checks the register, how often it's re-checked
  • Who is liable if a badged practitioner turns out unregistered
  • What happens to a doctor's patient data if verification lapses
Flag: the re-check date on the stamp isn't decoration. A one-time check at signup means a suspended licence stays badged for years.

Doctor · Step 02

One doctor, several clinics, different rules

Most established consultants here practise in more than one place — a hospital OPD in the morning, their own clinic in the evening, sometimes a third across town. Fees differ. Hours differ. Staff differ.

Consequences of getting this wrong

  • One shared calendar means double bookings across town
  • One shared fee means the wrong amount collected at the desk
  • One shared staff list means a hospital receptionist sees private-clinic patients
Design rule: the clinic — not the doctor — is the unit that owns a schedule, a fee, and a staff list. Permissions are scoped per location.

Doctor · Step 03

Least privilege, decided by the doctor

The doctor's account is the parent account — it owns the practice's patient data. Everyone else gets a role, and every role sees the minimum it needs to do its job.

The line that matters most

  • The receptionist needs the calendar, the name and the phone number
  • The receptionist does not need the diagnosis

That single boundary is both an ethical requirement and one of the strongest things you can show a doctor in a sales demo. It's also close to impossible to retrofit — so it goes in at v1.

Try it: switch between the three roles and watch the permission list change. Every view, edit and export is attributed to a named staff account in an audit log — and access dies the day someone leaves.

Doctor · Step 05

She has never met this patient before

And yet she opens the consultation knowing the allergy, the four-year hypertension history, the drug already tried, the recent labs, and today's blood pressure — taken minutes ago by her assistant.

What the screen prevents

  • Prescribing a penicillin to a penicillin-allergic patient
  • Re-ordering a lipid profile done last week
  • Re-starting a drug that already failed

The allergy flag isn't advisory — the interface refuses the contraindicated drug and says why.

The clinical case for the product: everything above the prescription pad came from other doctors, other labs, and the patient's own phone. No single clinic could have assembled it.

Doctor · Step 06

Retention, booked in the room

A follow-up scheduled while the patient is still sitting there converts far better than one suggested verbally and left to chance. This is the single highest-value habit the software can create.

The pitch to the doctor, in three numbers

  • No-shows down from 19% to 7% — reminders alone
  • 61% of patients return within 90 days — follow-ups booked in-room
  • Two open weeks visible ahead — so she can act before they empty
Open question: the revenue model changes what this screen optimises for. Per-booking commission pushes volume; a flat clinic subscription pushes retention. Decide before you build the dashboard.

Front desk · Step 01

The most important screen in the product

Not because it does much, but because of what it refuses to do. Saima runs the desk with everything she needs and nothing she doesn't. The clinical column simply isn't there for her.

Why this is stated out loud, not hidden

  • Staff should know the boundary, so they never feel blamed for a leak
  • Doctors need to see the boundary before they'll delegate at all
  • Patients deserve to know who at a clinic can read their file
Design detail: the locked panel is visibly present rather than silently absent. A blank space invites people to ask for access. A stated boundary settles it.
Least privilege

Front desk · Step 02

Walk-ins are not an edge case

A booking system designed only for appointments made in advance will be abandoned by lunchtime on day one. Plenty of patients simply arrive — and if the desk can't add them, the desk goes back to a paper register and the schedule stops reflecting reality.

What has to be true for adoption

  • Adding a walk-in takes one field and one click
  • Phone bookings can be entered by the receptionist, not just the patient
  • Every insertion is attributed and visible to the doctor
Try it: add Ali Raza. He slots in after the booked patients and carries Saima's name — the doctor sees who moved the day around.

Front desk · Step 03

Managing the room, not just the list

Every clinic in Pakistan runs late. The difference a platform can make isn't preventing that — it's making sure the person who finds out isn't the patient standing at the desk after two hours.

What one broadcast changes

  • Patients waiting at home stop leaving early
  • Some voluntarily reschedule, which drains the room
  • The receptionist stops absorbing anger she didn't cause
Try it: call Tariq in, then send the delay notice. Two patients reschedule themselves — the queue fixes itself without a single argument at the desk.

Front desk · Step 04

The receipt goes into her file too

Cash is still how most consultations are paid, so the flow has to treat it as first class rather than an exception. What changes is that the receipt is no longer only a piece of paper the patient loses.

Two people get something from this

Front desk · Step 05

The phone doesn't stop ringing, and that's fine

A platform that only accepts app bookings will show the doctor a half-empty calendar while the waiting room is full. Phone bookings must land in the same schedule, or the schedule is fiction.

Why this screen protects the whole system

  • One calendar, whatever channel the booking arrived through
  • The patient gets the same SMS confirmation and reminders
  • A patient with no smartphone is still fully served
Adoption insight: the receptionist is the person who can quietly kill this product by keeping a parallel paper register. Make her job easier than the register, and she becomes the reason it succeeds.

Assistant · Step 02

The most dangerous routine task in a clinic

Filing a report against the wrong patient is how a healthy person gets treated for someone else's condition. In a country where a dozen patients in one day may share a first name, name-matching alone is not safe.

How the interface reduces the risk

  • Match on the pending order, not the name — the doctor ordered this echo
  • Surface similar names deliberately instead of hiding them
  • Require an explicit confirmation, and log who confirmed
Design principle at work: where an error is irreversible and clinical, the interface should slow the user down on purpose.
Safety critical

Junior doctor · Step 03

Clinical depth, narrow scope

The junior doctor needs everything about the patient in front of him — the failed statin, the stent, the trend in LDL. What he doesn't get is the whole practice. Assignment, not seniority, is what opens a file.

Why assignment beats a blanket role

  • A trainee rotating out doesn't take 400 histories with him
  • The audit log answers "why did you open this file?" with "you were assigned it"
  • The senior doctor stays accountable for who sees what
Contrast with the front desk: Saima sees every patient's name and no clinical detail. Hamza sees a few patients in full depth. Two roles, two opposite shapes of access.

Junior doctor · Step 04

Delegation with a signature on it

A junior doctor drafts; the account owner issues. The patient only ever receives a prescription that a named, verified, registered doctor has signed — which is the whole reason the owner can safely let anyone else near the pad.

What the approval screen shows the senior

  • What changed from the last prescription, not just the new one
  • Which medicines are new to this patient
  • Whether anything conflicts with the recorded history

A co-sign that shows only a finished prescription isn't review — it's a rubber stamp in the bad sense.

Open question: whether co-sign is mandatory or a per-clinic setting. Some consultants will want it always on; some will trust senior registrars to issue directly. Make it the clinic's choice, and log which mode was active.

What this prototype is arguing

Twenty-two screens, four roles, one claim: the patient owns the record, the doctor owns the practice, and every other role gets the narrowest access that lets it do real work. Every design decision above follows from that split.

Ship in v1

Verified directory in one city, in-clinic booking, family profiles, patient-side upload, doctor account with the receptionist role, WhatsApp and SMS reminders, Urdu and English.

Hold for v2

Video consultations, home visits, record sharing, junior and assistant roles, lab integrations, billing, and the practice dashboard.

Answer before building

Verification liability, data-protection compliance, prescription legality, review moderation, revenue model, and which single city you saturate first.
